The Canvas Is Only the Surface

Why enterprise couldn't just be more of the same product

Miro's rapid growth brought a new class of customer. Smaller teams could adopt the product with very little administration. Large enterprises could not. They needed to manage thousands of users and teams, delegate administration, control access, understand adoption, meet security and compliance requirements, manage data across regions, and introduce new capabilities without increasing organizational risk.

Miro already had enterprise functionality, but it had grown feature by feature around a product originally optimized for teams. The challenge was no longer adding individual enterprise features. It was turning them into a system.

01

Build the Enterprise Foundation

From fragmented functionality to one coherent system

Miro had grown feature by feature for years, with no shared structure underneath. Users, admins, organizations, roles, permissions, security, settings, each had evolved on its own logic, shipped independently, without a system connecting them. The problem wasn't the UI. It was a missing mental model of what "enterprise" meant at Miro.

I led the work to define one: mapping the real relationships between every entity in the system, and validating that model against how existing customers actually understood and organized their own setups. This wasn't an information architecture exercise. It was a structural overhaul of how enterprise worked at Miro, end to end.

That model exposed the gap between what existed and what enterprise customers actually needed, and became the backlog for the org for the next several years. We divided the work into end-to-end journeys, sequenced by usage and ROI, and scoped the first phase to one year.

To deliver on that timeline, I built out the design team and helped stand up an engineering org focused specifically on end-to-end customer experience, rather than isolated features.

Cohesion over consistency. Does it behave like Miro? Is the mental model aligned? Visual inconsistency could wait. Contradictory behavior couldn't.

The other output of this phase was the control layer itself: the first system giving enterprise admins visibility across users, teams, activity, and adoption. It was built on granular authorization, designed to answer one question at any scope: who can do what, and where?

02

Turn Requirement Into Growth Lever

From prerequisite to business value

Once the foundation held, the mandate changed: turn administration from a cost of doing enterprise business into a lever for growing it. Admins, internally nicknamed "Miro Heroes", stopped being an afterthought and became the audience whose adoption decisions determined whether an account expanded.

The admin surface became a sales channel. The enterprise dashboard and invitation workflows became where admins saw adoption trends, discovered new features, and understood what Enterprise unlocked. Giving admins visibility into usage patterns, anomaly detection, and early threat signals turned out to be the strongest upgrade driver we had.

A/B testing on admin surfaces lifted enterprise conversion 30%.
Enterprise scale is not simply more users. It is more contexts, more policies, and more constraints.

Entering Japan meant rebuilding infrastructure, not just localizing UI. I spent a week in Tokyo working directly with the Japanese team, customers, and end users to understand what data residency actually required: storing data in Japan-based warehouses without breaking the real-time collaboration that makes Miro work.

Trust became a paid feature, not a compliance checkbox. Enterprises were adding confidential IP directly onto their boards, and were willing to pay a 30% premium to know it was protected. Resolving the freedom vs. control tension took six months and shipped as Enterprise Guard.

03

AI Changed What Control Meant

When the model changes, the rules change with it

When the AI wave hit, enterprise adoption lagged expectations, not because customers didn't want AI, but because AI didn't arrive as an isolated feature. It cut through every layer we'd already built: permissions, administration, security, compliance, governance, and the collaboration experience itself.

The architecture we'd designed for "who can access this content?" was not the right architecture for "what can intelligence do with this content?" Every system had to be rethought.

04

Build the Team

From headcount to capability

I joined Miro to a two-person design team. A skills matrix comparing existing strengths against what the future portfolio would demand became the hiring plan, interaction designers for the dashboards we lacked craft for, systems thinkers for problems that crossed permissions, administration, and governance.

The team went from zero to roughly twenty in four months. The matrix outlived hiring, leadership used it to staff projects deliberately and have contribution-based career conversations.

You may own an area, but you are building one system.
When the architecture of the product changes, the architecture of the organization has to change with it.
